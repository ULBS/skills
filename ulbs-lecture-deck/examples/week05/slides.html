Case 1 · Muscar.autoA fleet that never stops talking

What you see. Each blue dot is a vehicle; each gold dot is a telemetry message flying to the gateway. Drag the fleet size and watch the message rate and the data rate grow.

The company

  • Manufactures autonomous systems for vehicles
  • > 10,000 vehicles on the road now
  • > 200,000 expected by the end of the year
  • Needs to reliably receive telemetry and display data about the cars
Predict first: what is harder here, the dashboard or the data collection?

Muscar builds the software that drives cars. The cars report back constantly. Our job: receive it reliably and show it.

  • The case says 10,000 vehicles today and 200,000 by year end; the requirements later say 7,000 msgs/s. That implies 0.7 messages per vehicle per second. At 200,000 vehicles it would be 140,000 msgs/s. That tension is real: a good architect asks which number to design for. We keep 7,000 and flag the question.
  • Answer to the prediction: the dashboard is easy (10 users). The data collection is the hard part.

Case 1 · Step 2 · Ask about the NFRsYou are the architect: interview the client

What you see. The six questions the architect asks Muscar. Click a question to hear the client’s answer and what it means for the design; the requirements sheet on the right fills in.

Never design from the functional list alone. Six questions turn “a lot of data” into numbers.

Case 1 · Step 3 · Data volume7,000 messages a second is… how much?

What you see. The arithmetic behind the design. Change the fleet size, the message size or the retention and watch the numbers. The preset buttons load the slide’s scenario, a fleet 20 times larger, or a full year kept in the fast database.

1 message = 1 KB. 7,000 per second = 7 MB per second = about 25 GB an hour = about 605 GB a day = about 221 TB a year. That’s a lot.

  • Calculate on the board before pressing the preset: students should get 25.2 GB/h, 604.8 GB/day, 220.7 TB/year (decimal units).
  • Press “Fleet grows 20×”: 140,000 msgs/s, about 12 TB a day. The case does not cover this: it is the capacity-planning question you should raise with the client.
  • Press “Keep a full year hot”: the meter explodes. Leads to the next slide: retention.

Case 1 · Step 3 · Data volumeRetention: how long do we keep the data fast?

What you see. One square per day, the newest on the right. Teal days live in the fast operational database, gold days are older than the retention period and have moved to the archive. Start at day 1, press Next day, and watch the oldest teal day turn gold. Change the retention or choose Delete instead.

The retention period defines for how long records stay in the database, and what happens to them afterwards: deleted, or moved to an archive.

  • Motivation: keep the database from exploding and keep queries fast.
  • Muscar needs two kinds of data: operational near-real-time (location, speed) with 1 week of retention, and aggregated data for BI that is kept forever.
  • The 4 TB limit of the operational DB is simply 605 GB × 7 days.
  • Ask: what would break if we chose “delete”? (No history, no trends, no forecasts.)

Case 1 · Step 6 · Telemetry PipelineBuild or buy the queue?

What you see. A decision matrix for the pipeline: a queue that carries 7,000 messages per second between gateway and processor. Nothing exists in the company, so we looked around. Move the weights and see when Kafka stops winning.

Is there an existing queue in the company? No. Develop our own or use a third party? Let’s look around.

  • Kafka pros: very popular, handles massive amounts of data, supports high availability. Cons: complex set-up and configuration. The decision: Kafka.

Case 1 · Step 9 · RedundancyWhy a queue and why three instances

What you see. A live simulation. Cars send messages to three gateways, through the queue, to three processors and into the database. Click an instance to crash it, trigger a rush hour, or switch the queue off.

Redundancy for stateless tiers: several instances behind a load balancer. The queue is the shock absorber.

  • Telemetry gateway and processor each run as several instances behind a load balancer.
  • Experiments, in order: (1) default: healthy. (2) “Crash 2 processors”: capacity drops to 3,000/s, the queue grows, data is late but nothing is lost. (3) Switch to “No queue” and crash again: messages are lost. (4) Restart, then “Rush hour ×3”: the gateway tier (12,000/s) is the next bottleneck.
  • Capacities per instance are simulation parameters (gateway 4,000/s, processor 3,000/s), not measurements.

Case 1 · Step 6 · Processor and operational DBWhat do we store the messages in?

What you see. The processor validates and stores messages; the datastore must support schema-less messages, quick retrieval and no complex queries. Compare a document store, a relational database and an in-memory store.

Technology for the processor: already used in the system, fast, great Kafka support → Node.js again. For the datastore, look at what the data looks like.

  • The datastore must offer schema-less message support, quick retrieval and no complex queries → MongoDB.
  • Notice that “complex queries” has weight 1: the BI side handles them, on aggregated data, somewhere else.
  • Challenge: what if the client later asks for ad-hoc analytics on the operational DB? (Move it to the warehouse, do not complicate the operational store.)

Case 1 · Component · BI ApplicationViewer or BI? And whose job is BI?

What you see. Eight questions the business might ask. Send each one to the component that should answer it: the real-time Telemetry Viewer or the BI Application on top of the warehouse.

The viewer shows what is happening now. BI answers questions about the past and the future. And BI is not the architect’s job.

  • BI analyses telemetry and displays custom reports, trends and forecasts. “How many cars broke during the last month?” “What is the total distance the cars drove?”

Case 2 · Step 6 · Logging ServiceIs there a logging mechanism in the company?

What you see. Logging is very important and every other service uses it. Nothing exists in the company, so: build or buy? The ELK stack is powerful, but is it right here? Move the weights.

ELK is the famous answer: Elastic as the data store, Logstash to import logs, Kibana to view and filter. It is also overkill for us.

Case 2 · Steps 5–9 · Logging ServiceTwo instances and an “Is Alive?” check

What you see. The Logging Service is a background service: it polls the queue every few seconds, validates the records and stores them. Two instances share the work. Crash one and see what the other does.

Active / Active: both instances work, and each asks the other “Are you alive?”. When one stops answering, the other knows.

  • Steps for any component: decide the application type, the technology stack, the architecture. Stack: .NET and SQL Server (the team’s expertise). Architecture: polling, business logic, data access, data store.
  • Both instances read from one queue: each record goes to exactly one instance, so there are no duplicates. Raise the load above 30/s, then crash one: backlog grows. Redundancy is not the same as capacity.
  • No load balancer here: the instances pull from the queue by themselves.

Case 2 · Step 8 · API designVacation Service: set, get, reduce

What you see. HR sets the available days with PUT, an employee reduces them with POST. Reduce more days than are left and see the response: the slide lists only 200 for this endpoint.

Two roles, three endpoints. HR sets the available vacation days; employees reduce them.

  • PUT /api/v1/vacation/{empid} (200, 404), GET (200, 404), POST /{empid}/reduction (200).
  • Why a POST to /reduction and not a PUT with the new number? Two people could update at once; “reduce by N” is safe to repeat in order, “set to N” overwrites. Ask students to find a case where it matters.
  • Redundancy: two instances behind a load balancer, like the other services.

Case 2 · Steps 5–9 · Payment InterfacePay-day: how many copies must run?

What you see. On pay-day a timer wakes the Payment Interface. It reads every salary and writes one file, which the old payment system reads. Each employee must be paid exactly once. Pick a design on the left, press “Month-end”, and follow the four tasks.

Redundancy is not always “run more copies”: for a job that must run once, two active copies are a bug.

  • Design 1, one copy: crash it, fire the timer: nobody is paid. This is why we want a second copy.
  • Design 2, two active copies (what worked for logging): both write the file, everyone is paid twice.
  • Design 3, active + passive: only one copy runs. The standby watches “Is Alive”; after 3 s of silence it becomes active. Crash the active copy and fire after the failover: still paid once.
  • Why logging may be Active / Active: its copies share a queue, so each record reaches exactly one copy. The Payment Interface is triggered by a timer, so nothing shares the work.
  • The 3 s failover, 250 employees and €3,000 average salary are simulation parameters.

  • Observe when requests start failing and how the average request duration changes; note the thresholds

Part 2 · Compare with PostgreSQL

  • Switch the backend to PostgreSQL; adapt the data-loading script to it
  • Repeat the Locust tests (1, 2, 5, 10 or more users)
  • Compare average duration, failed requests, CPU/memory if possible
  • Short analysis: how does performance scale, what is the impact of the switch, why?
  • Post it on GitHub Classroom with Locust Statistics and Charts screenshots and your repository URL

The lab is the same method on your own system: numbers first (how many rows), then a decision (SQL vs a Python loop, SQLite vs PostgreSQL), then evidence (Locust).

  • Content taken from the lab assignment documents. The load-test document mentions ./gradlew bootRun and port 8080 for the backend; use whatever your team’s backend uses.
  • Deadlines and submission details are in the assignments on Classroom.

Wrap-upThree things to remember, and a checklist

What you see. The lessons of the two case studies, and a checklist you can tick against your own design. Your ticks are saved in this browser.

1 · Numbers first

Turn “a lot” into messages per second, GB per day, years of retention. The numbers choose the architecture.

2 · Context decides

Kafka or RabbitMQ, MongoDB or SQL Server, Node.js or .NET: scores describe the technology, weights describe your context.

3 · Plan for failure

Stateless: instances behind a load balancer. Timers and singletons: “Is Alive” with one active copy.

If you remember three things: numbers first, context decides, plan for failure.
